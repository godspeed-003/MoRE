\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Predictive quality}
\label{sec:quality}

Table~\ref{tab:main} collects every language result. On that task, recursion
alone is lowest-loss. MoR beats
MoE by $0.4329$ nats and beats MoRE by $0.1216$. Both recursive arms beat MoE
decisively, and the composition lands between them---the premise that routing
would \emph{add} to recursion in domain is not supported.

Two features of the table deserve to be read together. First, MoE and MoRE are
parameter-\emph{identical} at 5{,}584{,}908 while MoR sits $0.069\%$ lower, the
residual being the router MoR does not have---and that residual runs
\emph{against} the headline, since the arm with fewer parameters is the one that
wins. Second, MoRE's seed spread is $6.4\times$ tighter than MoR's in variance
(std $0.0134$ against $0.0340$; exact one-sided permutation test on the variance
ratio $p = 1/252$, that test's floor). \textbf{What that means is still open}:
these runs cannot separate a genuinely stabilising effect from the simpler
account that a model whose halting has collapsed to a near-constant has less left
to vary, and the second reading is the more parsimonious---MoRE's spread does
tighten across the three epochs ($0.034 \rightarrow 0.021 \rightarrow 0.013$)
while MoR's does not. We report the measurement and decline the interpretation.
(Exploratory and uncorrected.)

Fig.~\ref{fig:depth-curve} and the forced-depth sweep sharpen this. Both arms scale
with forced depth up to a point, in opposite directions:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1.5pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{MoR} improves from 7.36 nats at depth 1 to a minimum of 4.06 at
  depth 4, then \emph{degrades} to 4.99 at depth 7. It has a genuine interior
  optimum.
\item \textbf{MoRE} improves monotonically all the way to depth 7 (3.70), never
  turning over within the budget. It wants more depth than it has.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Is recursion actually adaptive?}
\label{sec:halting}

The mechanism claim rests on comparing each learned policy against a \emph{uniform}
policy, the cheapest competitor that ignores its input. Every uniform policy here
is the \emph{same trained weights} with depth forced at inference: each token runs
exactly $d$ steps and the output is the final state, in place of the
halting-weighted mixture of Eq.~\ref{eq:halt-w}. The comparison therefore measures
how much each trained model relies on its own learned policy. It is not a
comparison with a model \emph{trained} at a fixed depth, which could beat weights
forced onto a schedule they were not trained for. Two
comparisons matter, and they give different answers
for the two arms.

\textbf{Against the best uniform depth anywhere} (the strongest competitor): MoR's
learned policy at 3.5154 beats its best forced depth (4.0558 at $d{=}4$) by
\textbf{0.540 nats}. MoRE's learned policy at 3.6370 beats its best forced depth
(3.6971 at $d{=}7$) by \textbf{0.060 nats}.

\textbf{Against a depth-matched uniform policy}---forced to $d{=}7$, the integer
nearest both arms' measured mean depths (6.730 and 6.955), so the uniform policy
spends at least as much compute as the learned one: MoR's learned policy beats
forced-$d7$ (4.9863) by \textbf{1.471 nats}; MoRE's beats forced-$d7$ (3.6971) by
\textbf{0.060 nats}.

The depth-matched comparison is the one that bears on a mechanism claim, because
best-depth comparison conflates adaptivity with the amount of compute spent. On it,
removing MoR's learned policy costs 1.47 nats where removing MoRE's costs 0.06, a
factor of roughly 24. The honest summary is not that MoRE's depth mechanism is
inert; it is that adding routing left it almost entirely without effect.

\paragraph{The depth-matched control, as a general diagnostic.}
\textbf{A mean step count and an exit histogram---the two statistics this
literature almost always reports---do not establish that depth depends on the
input}: a policy that ignores its input reproduces any mean, and assigning the
learned depths to tokens at random reproduces the histogram as well. Forcing a
uniform policy at the learned mean depth is the cheapest test that holds compute
fixed. It costs one inference pass, needs no retraining, and separates two arms
whose mean depths are nearly identical (6.73 and 6.96 of 7). It is necessary
rather than sufficient: a small gap (MoRE) shows the learned policy does little
work, while a large gap (MoR) bounds what the policy contributes \emph{on weights
trained with it}. The sharper tests---the learned depths shuffled across tokens,
which holds the histogram fixed, and fixed-depth models trained from scratch---are
the next step. We recommend at least the uniform control before any model is
described as allocating computation adaptively.

The exit statistics say why MoRE's margin is so small. MoR early-exits
$18.8\% \pm 15.1\%$ of validation tokens with mean depth $6.730$ and a halt
remainder of $0.175$; MoRE force-exits $97.3\% \pm 0.8\%$ at the ceiling with mean
depth $6.955$ and remainder $0.655$. MoRE's halting has collapsed to ``run to
budget''.

\textbf{Depth by word class shows the difference directly}
(Table~\ref{tab:family-depth}). MoR's mean depth varies by 0.32 steps across six
part-of-speech families, deepest on function words and shallowest on nouns; MoRE's
varies by 0.009, $35\times$ less. Per token, MoR's depth correlates with its own
loss at $\rho = +0.2985 \pm 0.0654$, positive on \textbf{5/5} seeds. MoRE's
$\rho = -0.0230 \pm 0.0115$ is computed against a near-constant (per-token depth
standard deviation 0.43), so it is uninformative rather than evidence of bad
allocation---which is why the family table matters: it measures the absence of
allocation instead of inferring it from a correlation with a constant.

MoR's early-exit rate is the seed-unstable quantity ($6.4\%$--$42.2\%$), and its
instability is informative: the two seeds that exit most are the two with the
\emph{worst} loss. Per-token depth tracks each model's own loss on every seed, but

part, and it costs loss.

\label{sec:ood}

scores each arm against the inference FLOPs it costs.

\paragraph{Reading the two FLOPs rows.} All arms share an output projection worth
$79.9\%$ of MoE's per-token compute. Table~\ref{tab:main} uses the total
accounting; the per-FLOPs figures quoted here are the excluded ones, and
Appendix~\ref{app:flops} derives both.

\textbf{In domain on the language task, all three arms are on the frontier; out
of domain, MoRE dominates.} Fig.~\ref{fig:pareto} plots loss against inference
compute. In domain no arm Pareto-dominates another: MoE is cheapest, MoR is
best, MoRE sits between
them, so MoRE's in-domain claim is a \emph{ratio}---the most quality per unit of
compute---and not a position the others fail to occupy. We state the weaker
reading explicitly, because it is the one a reviewer can check.

Out of domain the relationship changes in kind rather than degree. On held-out
text from the Pile \citep{gao2020pile} MoRE reaches 7.212 nats against MoR's 7.423
and MoE's 7.946, at $2.19\times$ MoE's FLOPs against MoR's $8.87\times$---so MoRE
is both cheaper and better than MoR, and \textbf{MoR is Pareto-dominated}. Its
advantage over MoE is $0.734$ nats against MoR's $0.523$, i.e. MoRE captures
$140\%$ of MoR's out-of-domain gain against $72\%$ in domain. The in-domain
ordering inverts, and this is the one respect in which composing the two
mechanisms does something neither does alone. The MoRE$-$MoR gap ($-0.211$ nats)
is exploratory, at $p = 0.024$ uncorrected; one MoRE seed overlaps the best MoR seed.

\textbf{What the out-of-domain set is.} The $1{,}500$ scored blocks come from 943 documents of a $10{,}000$-document Pile sample, at most 512 tokens each, Wikipedia excluded. An earlier unconstrained draw of the same sample gave the same inversion (MoRE 7.573, MoR 7.802, MoE 8.272), so it is not an artefact of one document (Appendix~\ref{app:ood-provenance}).



\textbf{It gets there far more cheaply, on a different axis.} MoR pays
$8.87\times$ MoE's inference FLOPs; MoRE pays $2.19\times$---$25\%$ of MoR's total
inference compute. Excluding the output projection all three arms share, those
become $6.92\times$ and $17\%$ (Table~\ref{tab:main}), and MoRE buys $0.045$ nats
per FLOPs-multiplier against MoR's $0.011$: $4.2\times$ the quality per unit
compute on validation loss, $8.1\times$ out of domain. \textbf{The two axes are
never substituted}: at matched parameters, on the language task, MoR is
lowest-loss by $0.1216$ nats, at our resolution floor, while per unit of
inference compute MoRE is ahead---so ``comparable to MoR'' is only ever a
\emph{per-FLOP} statement here, never an absolute-quality one.

FLOPs, not wall-clock, is the compute measure, and the throughput row shows how
much of the FLOP advantage our implementation turns into speed. Timed on one GPU
for all fifteen checkpoints, MoRE runs $1.46\times$ as fast as MoR (310 against 212
blocks/s), a real but much smaller margin than its $4.05\times$ FLOP advantage,
because per-token expert dispatch is latency-bound; against MoE it runs at $0.28\times$
the speed for $2.19\times$ the FLOPs. A batched-dispatch implementation should
realise more of the FLOP advantage; we claim only the measured fraction.

\textbf{Grammatical competence is real but modest, and routing does not buy it.} On BLiMP \citep{warstadt2020blimp} both recursive arms beat MoE (58.74\% and 58.36\% against 56.63\%) and MoR is not distinguishable from MoRE ($p = 0.71$), so recursion buys measurable syntactic sensitivity and routing adds none. Appendix~\ref{app:blimp} reports it in full, with EWoK, on which every arm sits at chance.
\textbf{Both recursive arms gain most where prediction is hardest, and least where context is shortest.} By target-token frequency decile the MoRE$-$MoE advantage grows from $-0.147$ nats on the most frequent to $-0.374$ on the rarest (MoR: $-0.188$ to $-0.527$), so neither gain is the uniform shift a pure capacity effect would give. By position, both arms gain most where context is \emph{longest}---disconfirming the usual motivation for recursion (Appendix~\ref{app:stratified}).

\subsection{A task built for the architecture}
\label{sec:arithmetic}

The language task can show that the composition does not add, but not whether
routing and recursion are being used for their intended purpose: natural language
supplies no operation label and no difficulty axis. The arithmetic task of
\S\ref{sec:arith-task} supplies both by construction, and on it---at this
protocol's fixed three-epoch budget---the in-domain ordering reverses.

Two claims of different strength. \textbf{Against MoE the gap is decisive}: MoRE
is $0.2403$ nats lower, and the seed ranges are disjoint. \textbf{Against MoR it
is not resolvable at five seeds}: MoRE is lower in four of five seeds and its
between-seed spread is $4.8\times$ smaller, but the mean gap of $0.055$ sits
inside MoR's own spread of $0.085$. We therefore claim a better mean and a
tighter distribution, not a win.

The stability difference is the more informative result. MoR's seeds settle on
different halting policies---one chose a mean depth of $2.11$ and scored
$0.393$, another chose $4.11$ and scored $0.203$---and its loss falls
monotonically in the depth each seed chose. MoRE holds a policy near four steps
in every seed and its loss stays inside a narrow band. One MoR seed also paged
($12.8$\,GiB on an $8.2$\,GiB card), so we quote no arithmetic throughput.

\paragraph{Is the halting policy doing work?} We force every token to each depth
$1,\dots,7$ at inference and rescore the same checkpoint, as on language. The
identity check holds: at $T=1$ MoE's forced loss equals its learned loss to four
decimals in all five seeds, so that branch returns what it must. This control
re-evaluates the best-validation checkpoint, and its learned loss ($0.188$) is
therefore not the final-epoch figure of Table~\ref{tab:arith}; the two are never
combined.

Depth-control detail---the depth-matched and best-uniform margins, and why the two rows are a bracket rather than two contradictory measurements---is in Appendix~\ref{app:arith-depth}.

\textbf{Routing makes recursion stable and supplies the quality; the halting head
is input-dependent but not load-bearing.} Against the depth-matched control the
two recursive arms are indistinguishable on the mean ($0.842$ against $0.837$),
so on arithmetic the learned policy is worth as much to MoRE as to MoR---the
opposite of the language result, where the same control gave $0.060$ against
$1.471$. Against the best fixed depth, however, MoRE's margin collapses to
$0.095$: a constant depth of five or six recovers most of what the policy buys,
so the composition's advantage is routing's, not halting's. MoR is the arm whose
policy is hard to replace ($0.221$), and its mean matched margin is carried by
one seed whose halting collapsed. Swapping the halt head for a constant leaves
MoRE far ahead of MoE and still ahead of MoR. We do not compare the two tasks'
margins as numbers---different vocabularies, scoring rules and scales---but a fixed
depth recovers all of MoRE's $0.060$ on language and most of its $0.842$ on
arithmetic, so the language reading was specific to that setting.

\paragraph{Four limits.} No arm generalises to unseen iteration counts (all arms are at chance on $k=5,6,7$ while the seen counts are learned); depth does not track difficulty (MoRE's mean depth is flat, $4.43$--$4.45$, while accuracy falls from $0.998$ at $k=2$ to $0.661$ at $k=4$); routing does not align with the seven-group prior (AMI $0.031$ against MoE's $0.107$); and the depth policy is replaceable, as above. Appendix~\ref{app:arith-limits} reports each with its numbers.
