\begin{abstract}
Conditional computation chooses either \emph{which} parameters process a token
---sparse mixture-of-experts (MoE)---or \emph{how much} computation it receives
---adaptive-depth weight-tied recursion (MoR). Composing them should inherit
both. We test whether it pays: one engine, three
configurations, parameters matched to 0.069\% ($\approx$5.58M), bit-identical
inputs, five seeds, exact randomisation tests under a pre-registered correction,
on WikiText-103.

All three arms lie on the quality/compute frontier, and the composition (MoRE)
buys the most quality per unit of compute. It improves on MoE by $7.9\%$
validation loss at $2.19\times$ MoE's inference FLOPs, where recursion alone needs
$8.87\times$: $72\%$ of recursion's quality gain for $25\%$ of its compute. Out of domain it is stronger still---it
\emph{dominates} recursion alone, reaching lower loss at a quarter of the compute.

It is not lowest-loss in domain. Recursion alone leads by $0.1216$ nats,
significant at our resolution floor, and adding routing improves neither
in-domain quality nor routing quality over MoE; we keep the quality and compute
axes separate. We trace the deficit to a competition for one per-token budget,
excluding two rival explanations. Most transferably: a mean step count and an exit histogram cannot
establish input-dependent depth, since a uniform policy forced to the learned mean
reproduces both. Scoring against that control separates our recursive arms by a
factor of 24.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Broader Impact Statement}

This work trains 5.6M-parameter language models on WikiText-103, a public corpus
derived from Wikipedia, and evaluates them on public benchmarks. No human
subjects, annotation, or personally identifying data are involved, and the models
are far below the scale at which generative misuse is a practical concern. The
realistic effect of the work runs through other researchers' decisions rather
than through deployment: we report a negative result about an architecture
combination, and a diagnostic---scoring any learned depth policy against a
depth-matched uniform policy before describing it as adaptive---that should save
compute otherwise spent on a mechanism that is not paying for itself. The
asymmetric risk is the one worth naming: a null obtained at 5.6M parameters could
discourage work that would succeed at scale, which is why
\S\ref{sec:limitations} states the scale objection as a falsifiable prediction
with the measurement that would overturn it rather than as a hedge. The corpus
inherits the demographic and topical biases of Wikipedia, which we did not
measure and which bear on any downstream use of these checkpoints.
